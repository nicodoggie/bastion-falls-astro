**Cubifabrids** are low-bodied, heavily plated earth elementals native to the volcanic vents of the
[Northern Maltreks](../locations/the-maltreks.mdx). They graze on sulfurous, silica-rich mineral
aggregates around fumaroles and hydrothermal fissures. Their bodies incorporate sulfur into a
heat-producing metabolism and silica into a self-repairing ceramic shell. Bismuth and related heavy
material are instead separated, refined, and expelled through an organ commonly called the **ingot
womb**.

The two best-known cubifabrids are the social **cubesmith**, _Cubifaber ordinatus_, and the more
territorial **ingotback**, _Cubifaber effusor_. Their raised-language genus preserves an older folk
belief that the regular bismuth blocks found near cubesmith dens were the deliberate work of
subterranean artisans. Comparative anatomy later extended the cubesmith's name to its live-casting
relative.

## The Ingot Womb

An ingot womb is a thick-walled mineral organ connected to the cubifabrid's furnace-gut. It removes
bismuth from digested aggregate, transports it through heated channels, and gathers it around a
reusable crystallization matrix. The organ is not reproductive. Its common name arose among miners
who opened carcasses and found several metal blocks at different stages of formation inside a
chamber that seemed to gestate and deliver them.

The organ's ancestral function is safe purgation. Loose bismuth crystals would branch through and
perforate the creature's internal channels; concentrating the metal into compact forms allows it to
pass without injury. Cubesmiths complete this process internally, producing regular blocks.
Ingotbacks retain bismuth in a molten or semi-molten state and complete the casting outside the
body.

## Cubesmiths

_Cubifaber ordinatus_ lives in colonies around broad vent fields. Families arrange cooled cubes in
communal middens, along den boundaries, and around nursery galleries. Their apparent workmanship
gave the species its common and scholarly names. Although generally tolerant of people who keep
beyond the nursery boundary, an adult can brace against the ground and launch a prepared block from
its terminal tract. Miners call this defensive discharge a **cloacal ballista**.

Midden gathering can sustain a long relationship between a colony and a nearby settlement.
Experienced gatherers watch the family's movements, approach only cooled and abandoned deposits, and
avoid taking blocks used to mark active passages. Regular disturbance, theft from nursery
structures, or hunting turns an otherwise docile colony defensive.

## Ingotbacks

_Cubifaber effusor_ retains processed bismuth in a heated reservoir and expels it across intruders
and terrain. The metal lands dull and molten, then hardens into angular, iridescent castings.
Ingotbacks use these discharges to seal passages, divide attackers, and defend routes into the
family den.

Ingotbacks live in small family units rather than as strictly solitary animals. One adult ordinarily
patrols the perimeter while the others forage, maintain galleries, and move the young away from
danger. Miners usually call this defender the **alpha**. Naturalists prefer **warding adult**, since
observation shows that warding is a rotating form of dangerous labor rather than a fixed dominance
rank. The role may change with health, casting reserves, age, and the needs of the family.

Young cubifabrids cover a broad developmental range. Newly formed young have porous shells and no
controlled casting. Older juveniles may leak or spit hot metal when frightened. Armored subadults
have functional ingot wombs but produce shorter and less stable castings. Fully developed foraging
adults can cast reliably, while a well-provisioned warder has the thickest plating and largest
defensive reservoir.

## Age, Family, and Death

Elderly cubifabrids are uncommon. Territorial challenges, competition between families, ingot-womb
impaction, and declining control over crystallization all contribute to their scarcity. Human
hunting has distorted the pattern further: old warders are exposed at vent boundaries, and mature
ingot-womb matrices command the highest prices. Communities that protect cubifabrids report elders
remaining with families after leaving the warding role, contradicting the widespread mining claim
that every old “alpha” is violently expelled.

A cubifabrid's death ends the elemental maintenance that holds its heated composite body together.
Fresh carcasses cool unevenly, vent sulfurous gas, and produce audible pings as ceramic plates crack
or spall. Miners say such a body is still **ringing** and wait before approaching it. Once cooled,
the porous internal matrix crumbles and leaves a hollow ceramic shell reinforced by glassy seams.

Families reclaim much of this material. Plates may be consumed for silica, fitted into unstable vent
walls, or used to shelter young. A long-serving warder's shell can gradually become part of the den
entrance. Whether repeated guarding and touching of particular remains constitutes mourning remains
a matter of naturalist debate.

## Material Uses

Naturally purged bismuth is a useful specialty metal rather than a precious one. Its purity and low
working temperature make it valuable for casting, alloys, pigments, and alchemical reagents. The
carcass offers additional materials:

- dense outer plates provide refractory ceramic for kilns, furnaces, and crucibles;
- smooth inner glaze can be remade into chemically resistant **ventglass**;
- thin joint scales serve as overlapping heat guards;
- glassy silica seams fracture into extremely sharp knives, scrapers, and points.

Legitimate shellers salvage naturally dead animals only after the body has finished ringing.
Hunter-miners often carry seamknives and shell armor made from earlier kills, equipment that living
cubifabrids may recognize as a threat.

## Kalassarian Use

During the Kalassarian Golden Age, Northern Maltrek settlements developed a mixed cubifabrid
economy. Some tended vents, provisioned mineral aggregate, gathered abandoned middens, and salvaged
naturally cooled shells. Others hunted cubifabrids or supplied an illegal trade in womb-cut metal
and intact organs. Near the end of the period, a small number of alchemical institutions began
experimenting with captivity and the use of living cubifabrids as biological refineries.

The surviving evidence is uneven. A tended midden, hunting camp, shellworking floor, ritual image,
or experimental enclosure preserves only one part of the relationship. Structures first attributed
entirely to Kalassarian industry have sometimes proved to contain walls and shelters assembled by
the animals themselves. Conversely, sites remembered as harmless midden grounds may conceal opened
carcasses and alchemical workrooms.

Cubifabrids never vanished from the Northern Maltreks. Local gatherers and craftspeople continued to
use their middens and shells after the wider Kalassarian system fell from memory. Current
scholarship is rediscovering the historical importance of practices that mountain communities
continued in more ordinary forms.

## Alchemical Exploitation

The greatest alchemical demand is not for bismuth but for the ingot womb. The organ separates one
substance from a mixed aggregate, controls heat and phase, establishes a repeatable crystal matrix,
and survives repeated casting cycles. Alchemists have sought to reproduce these functions in the
hope of purifying magical alloys, preserving unstable reagents, or separating a magical property
from its native tissue.

Midden collection can support a cubifabrid family for generations; removing an ingot womb kills the
animal and often destroys the family structure protecting the vent. This difference has produced a
long conflict between midden gatherers, vent-tenders, carcass salvagers, hunters, and the organ
traders known as **womb-cutters**.
