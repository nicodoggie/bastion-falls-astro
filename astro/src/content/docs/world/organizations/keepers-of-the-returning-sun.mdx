The **Keepers of the Returning Sun** are a small initiatory tradition devoted to Thrakel, the sun
god, in rural districts of the former [Kingdom of Anasai](kingdom-of-anasai.mdx). They have no
public temple network or recognised central priesthood. Most neighbours who know a Keeper know a
farmer, a healer, or a relative who observes certain household customs, rather than an adherent of a
surviving solar religion.

Their name refers less to sunrise than to an obligation: what the sun gives must be passed onward.
Warmth, food, skilled work, and magical power are understood as gifts temporarily entrusted to
living bodies. To return a gift is to make it useful beyond oneself. The Keepers do not ordinarily
mean that it must be sent back into the sky.

## Circles and Custodians

The tradition consists of household circles connected by personal introductions. A circle meets
where its members can gather privately: a kitchen after the day's work, an orchard shelter, or a
room lent for the occasion. Its continuity depends on people rather than a consecrated building.

Each circle recognises a **custodian of the bowl**, responsible for its ritual vessel, instruction,
and the admission of new members. The office confers no authority over other circles. Healers may
be custodians, but healing skill and religious standing are distinct; a capable healer need not be
an initiate, and a custodian need not be capable of healing.

Circles exchange visitors and teachings without maintaining a complete register of membership.
They sometimes disagree over whether another circle has preserved a mystery or merely retained
its gestures. Their small scale and discretion make both their numbers and their distribution
difficult to establish.

## The Blood of the Sun

The Keepers preserve a religious interpretation of *thraker*, **Sun's Blood** or **Blood of
Thrakel**, associated with the ancestors of the [Hick](../ethnicities/hick.mdx) peoples. They regard
the liver's processing of magic as the bodily counterpart of the sun's generosity: power enters a
living person, is changed there, and can pass outward in a working.

Bile's colour supplies an important image in their teaching, but not a universally agreed doctrine.
Some custodians describe it as a material witness to Thrakel's presence. Others call it a likeness,
warning against confusing the sign with the power it signifies. Neither interpretation makes every
yellow substance sacred or every use of magic an act of worship.

The related expressions *thrak'er*, to become jaundiced or to yellow, and *thrak*, yellow, also
circulate outside the tradition. Their use does not establish religious allegiance. Many people
who know a remedy for "letting the sun out" have never heard of the Keepers.

## The Covered Bowl

The characteristic rite uses an ordinary bowl, water, and a cloth. The bowl is covered while a
custodian speaks of gifts received. Those present then name something they have passed onward
since their previous gathering. An admission that nothing was given is permitted; inventing a
worthy deed for the rite is considered worse than having none to name.

The cloth is lifted, and the water is shared for washing hands before a meal. Where a member can
perform a small working, warming the water may accompany the rite. The ceremony remains complete
without it. Cultivating a crop or tending another person is not considered a lesser religious act
because it produces no visible magic.

For an initiation, the candidate brings the bowl rather than receiving one from the circle. It
must be an object they are willing to let others use. During the meal it passes beyond their seat;
they must ask for it back like anyone else. Custodians explain the exercise as a lesson in the
difference between contributing something and purchasing authority over its recipients.

The candidate learns prayers addressed explicitly to Thrakel and undertakes to keep the identities
of fellow initiates private. The prayers, their interpretation, and recognition between circles
constitute the mysteries. They are not required of people who receive treatment or share a meal.

## Healing and Retention

An inherited diagnosis attributes jaundice to magical power retained within the liver. Treatment
seeks to release it, preferably with a healer's guidance. Some Keepers continue to teach this as a
literal explanation of the illness; others preserve it as the traditional language of treatment
without regarding it as sufficient to explain every patient.

The treatment can have a genuine magical effect. A patient's released, processed magic can interact
with a healer's intent and the healer's own magic, reinforcing the healing. An unguided release may
also produce healing accidentally. Neither result establishes that failure to release magic caused
the illness in the first place.

This distinction is contested within the circles. Custodians who defend the retention diagnosis
point to successful treatments as evidence of restored flow. Healers who doubt it distinguish the
cause of an illness from the means by which it can be relieved. Both may perform outwardly similar
rites and invoke the same god.

The disagreement becomes consequential when treatment fails. Some practitioners seek another
healer or explanation; others urge a further attempt at release. Accusations that a patient is
secretly withholding power are rejected by circles that regard the admission of need as sufficient.
Such accusations nevertheless occur, and the tradition has no central authority capable of settling
them for everyone.

## Antiquity and Survival

The Keepers claim to preserve mainland solar mysteries older than Anasai itself. Their present
existence does not establish an uninterrupted organization from that period. Inherited household
practices, recovered language, and later religious interpretation may all contribute to what a
circle now teaches.

Accounts of the years when magical workings were unavailable commonly emphasise the meal, the
shared bowl, and useful work. Some custodians hold that these practices carried the mysteries
through the interruption. Others acknowledge that prayers and explanations may have been recovered
or rearranged afterward. No agreed succession of custodians connects the earliest claimed teachers
to the present circles.

The former kingdom supplies a geographic description, not a political programme. The circles do
not collectively seek its restoration, nor does their presence identify a district's inhabitants
as Thrakel worshippers. They are a very small religious minority within a much wider inheritance
of words and household medicine.
