_The Night of Borrowed Hands_ is the most widely recognized name for a family of annual local
traditions found along the river corridor surrounding
[Acqua Termina](../locations/acqua-termina.mdx). No village possesses a universally accepted form of
the observance. Neighboring communities inherit, exchange, discard, and reinterpret its practices,
while retaining a common principle: invited contribution does not transfer authorship of the work.

## The Threshold Cords

In one influential branch of the tradition, households place unfinished or damaged work at their
thresholds as evening falls. A colored cord states what neighbors and strangers may do:

- **green:** add one small piece of work;
- **gold:** offer advice or materials, but do not work upon the object;
- **white:** look, remember, and leave it untouched.

No contributor may finish another person's work outright. At dawn, each maker examines what was
offered and chooses what to keep, undo, alter, or incorporate. Removing a contribution carries no
insult. The cords invite help under terms chosen by the maker; they do not create a debt or grant
the helper any claim over the finished work.

The three colors are neither universal nor demonstrably original. Some settlements use knots to
distinguish hands, materials, and counsel. Others require every contributor to leave one part
visibly unfinished. In some places the maker's dawn decisions are private, while elsewhere the
public removal of an unwanted addition is treated as an ordinary and honorable conclusion to the
rite.

## The River Traditions

Upstream settlements commonly preserve threshold observances centered on tools, furniture, masonry,
and household work. In floodplain villages, eligible objects may instead be gathered in shared lanes
or raised workyards when private thresholds are temporary or difficult to reach.

In Acqua Termina, the custom extends to damaged houses and public-facing workshops. Downriver and
port communities have adapted related permissions to boats, nets, sails, and communal landing
stages. At the edges of the tradition's range, only a cord, a dawn undoing, or a children's game may
survive, sometimes attached to an observance no longer called the Night of Borrowed Hands.

Villages readily borrow an appealing practice from their neighbors while continuing to accuse those
same neighbors of performing the festival backwards. Several communities claim to preserve the
proper form, but none can demonstrate an unbroken or unaltered lineage.

## Flood and Reconstruction

The traditions are generally held to predate the catastrophic flooding of Acqua Termina in 1275,
when undead forces destroyed the city's flood-control infrastructure. Their earlier history remains
uncertain. Household accounts commonly connect them to recurring flood damage, displacement, and
piecemeal rebuilding, circumstances in which accepting another person's labor could be necessary
without surrendering ownership of a home, tool, or livelihood.

The reconstruction after 1275 transformed the observance. Displaced households and traveling workers
carried practices between settlements; local forms combined; and the right to remove an unwanted
contribution gained particular force in a city where extensive rebuilding might otherwise allow
helpers, guilds, landlords, or officials to treat assistance as a claim upon survivors' homes.

Antiquarians disagree over whether the river traditions descend from one older rite, arose
independently under similar conditions, or acquired their resemblance through borrowing after 1275.
Their evidence consists of recopied household records, replaced cords, rebuilt thresholds, displaced
workshop stories, and customs that continued changing while they were being documented. The history
of the Night of Borrowed Hands therefore remains much like the work displayed during the festival:
inherited, altered by many hands, and never placed beyond revision.
