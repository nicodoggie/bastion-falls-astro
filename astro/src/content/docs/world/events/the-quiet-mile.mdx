_The Quiet Mile_ is a small annual festival observed by a cluster of levee settlements in the
[Rannek Delta](../locations/rannek-delta.mdx). It coincides with the first managed high-water test
before the season when tides and runoff place the greatest strain on the embankments, drainage
works, and reclaimed fields of the [Rannek Marches](../locations/rannek-marches.mdx). Sluices admit
enough water to press against the works and reveal seepage, but the channels can still be lowered
and marked defects repaired before dangerous water arrives.

## The Quiet Hour

Once the water reaches the agreed inspection height, the gates are held steady and the participating
settlements suppress every avoidable noise. Mills stop turning. Carts remain off the levee roads.
Bells, music, hammering, public calls, and ordinary conversation cease. Even visitors are asked to
wait rather than enter or leave by carriage.

During this silence, households and work crews walk inherited stretches of embankment. The customary
"mile" is a communal circuit rather than an exact unit of distance; some assigned stretches are far
shorter, while outlying crews may inspect several miles of works.

Inspectors walk slowly and use simple tools suited to local ground conditions. They press bare feet
or listening cups against packed earth, watch shallow bowls for unexpected trembling, probe soft
patches, and look for fresh seepage, burrowing animals, displaced stones, or changes in the sound of
water behind a gate. Colored pegs mark anything that should be examined or repaired after the hour.
Children usually accompany experienced relatives and are expected to learn the difference between
harmless surface water and the faint movement of water beneath an embankment.

Finding a flaw does not spoil the festival. It is treated as fortunate that the earth spoke while
there was still time to answer. Concealing a defect to preserve a perfect inspection is considered
both shameful and dangerously foolish.

## The Breaking of the Quiet

When every crew has returned or signaled that its stretch is accounted for, the quiet ends all at
once. Sluice bells ring, people stamp on the raised roads, pipes and drums begin, vendors call over
one another, and arguments deliberately become louder than necessary. The sudden noise is the part
most children anticipate.

Repairs marked during the inspection begin as ordinary work once the test water is lowered, rather
than as ceremonial punishment. A serious discovery may shorten the feast, but it gives the
settlement something specific to organize around before dangerous water arrives.

Visitors sometimes mistake the quiet hour for mourning, penance, or a religious observance. Locals
usually refuse to correct them until the silence has ended, after which increasingly contradictory
explanations are a common form of entertainment.

## Origin

Local tradition credits the Quiet Mile to a child who heard water moving beneath an older, noisier
celebration and warned the settlement before an embankment failed. Different settlements name a
different child, levee, and threatened flood basin. The historicity of any one version is uncertain,
but the lesson remains consistent: a community that lives behind walls of earth must occasionally
be quiet enough to hear them failing.
