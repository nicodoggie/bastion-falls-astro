*An essay by Miren Tal, a student of words and household medicine.*

## A Colour with Something Inside It

A yellow leaf and a yellow face need not invite the same advice. One announces a season; the other
may bring a healer to the bedside. Yet a word can travel between them without remembering which it
visited first.

Consider *thrak*, the [Early Hick](../languages/hickic/seneran/early-hick/index.mdx) colour word,
and the fuller *thraker*. The latter has the appearance of a verb. Its proposed ancestry, however,
contains not a verbal ending but blood: *mar*, with the older form reconstructed as *maru*.
The first element belongs to *thrakel*, the sun, and to Thrakel, the sun god. The old expression is
thus understood as **Sun's Blood**, or **Blood of Thrakel**.

There is a pleasing trap here for the student who believes that every familiar ending has always
been an ending. In this account, the final *r* is a remnant of the word for blood. Speakers later
heard a verbal formation where their ancestors had made a compound. The form was brought into line
with the verbalizer *-'er*, giving *thrak'er*, and an independent *thrak* could be inferred from it.
What looks like an elementary colour word may therefore be the youngest member of the family.

Such an account does not require *maru* to have undergone extraordinary adventures in isolation.
A word may lose a final vowel and remain otherwise recognisable, while its participation in a
longer, differently stressed expression leaves almost nothing of it. Compounds are poor custodians
of their smaller inhabitants.

The bodily sense makes the reinterpretation more intelligible. To yellow is also to become
jaundiced. A term associated with the sun's blood could come to describe that change in a person,
be understood as a verb, and yield a colour root subsequently applied to cloth, flowers, and leaves.
This is a proposed history, not permission to assign every occurrence of yellow to the same belief.

## The Mainland Explanation

Among the ancestral mainland [Hick](../ethnicities/hick.mdx) beliefs considered here, the liver
belongs to an account of magic and the sun. It is not merely a piece of the body beside the seat of
some higher faculty. It is where magical power is processed, and where the sun's contribution to
human life may be understood to reside.

Bile supplies a particularly persuasive neighbour for this idea: a yellow substance associated
with the liver, close to both the bodily condition and the sacred colour. The precise ancestry of
its name need not be settled to recognise the attraction of connecting blood, bile, and magical
release. Nor must these substances be regarded as identical. A resemblance can sustain a doctrine
without becoming a complete anatomy.

The folk diagnosis follows: a person becomes jaundiced because they have failed to let their magic
out. What should flow has been kept within. The yellowing is interpreted as evidence of retention. A
household may explain the illness as the sun held in the liver, rather than the sun absent from it.

This is a particular tradition, not a belief to be assigned to every Hick-speaking people. Its
relation to later mainland practices, and the extent of its survival through periods when magic
was unavailable, require separate histories. An old explanation and a present working are not,
merely by resembling each other, proof of uninterrupted practice.

## A Cure That Can Outlive Its Explanation

The remedy seeks release. A patient is encouraged to let processed magic pass outward, preferably
under a healer's guidance. An unguided release may happen to produce healing. When it does, the
family has more than a consoling story: something has actually helped the patient.

Guidance makes an important difference. The patient's released magic can interact with the
healer's intent and the healer's own magic, reinforcing the healing. The patient contributes to
the working rather than simply enduring a ceremony while another person performs the useful part.
The healer's skill is consequently real, even when their explanation of the patient's illness is
mistaken.

A practitioner may learn to recognise the release, to guide it, and to join their own working to
it without abandoning the language of obstruction and flow. There is no need to imagine a secret
fraud beneath every effective household treatment. Skill can be taught more accurately than its
explanation.

But a cure is not a demonstration of a cause. The fact that releasing magic sometimes heals a
jaundiced patient does not establish that withholding magic made them jaundiced. Nor does the
failure of a treatment establish that the patient was unwilling to release it. The same practice
that offers relief can, in careless hands, become an accusation against the person it was meant
to help.

There are two questions to ask at the bedside: what has made this person ill, and what might help
them recover? A tradition may answer the second better than the first.

## What the Word Keeps

The connection is valuable even to those who do not accept the solar explanation. A speaker can
use a word for yellow without worshipping Thrakel; a healer can guide a release without believing
bile to be imprisoned sunlight. Language carries old interpretations farther than assent does.

Landscape names offer another possible home for such expressions. Golden woodland might be
called the sun's blood, while the same worn-down expression elsewhere describes an ailing body.
Whether a landscape name preceded the bodily sense or followed it must not be decided from the
resemblance alone.

Beauty and illness are awkward neighbours, but a language is not obliged to separate them for our
comfort. The sun in a leaf may be admired. The sun in a liver may be urged to leave. Between those
two judgements lies a history worth keeping—and a patient worth treating without first requiring
them to understand it.
