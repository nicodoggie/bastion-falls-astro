*An excerpt from an essay by Aveline Vey, occasional contributor to the Encyclopaedia of Thera.*

There are institutions which discover the public only when the public has become difficult to
ignore. The Ministry of Science, to its credit, has found a more efficient route: it has arranged
for the public to discover itself in the pages of
[*Legally Bare*](../organizations/legally-bare.mdx), and has called the encounter an education.

This is an arrangement worth taking seriously, if only because serious persons have rushed to take
it unseriously. The magazine's defenders say that its appeal is an invitation into useful knowledge.
This is not impossible. A reader may arrive for a portrait and remain for a discussion of practical
anatomy, lunar salts, or the calibration of an inexpensive charm. Those who have spent time among
students know that attention is not a pure substance. It attaches itself to a map, a specimen, a
rumour from a distant port, the prospect of an examination, a beautiful face, a dreadful one, or any
combination the lecturer has failed to foresee.

The question is not whether an adult may be enticed toward learning. Adults plainly may. Nor is it
whether knowledge must wear a solemn coat before it deserves to be called knowledge. A great deal of
useful instruction has been conducted in workshops, kitchens, ships' holds, and rooms that no
respectable catalogue would think to index.

The ministry's particular arrangement bears examination for another reason. In much of the magazine,
the columnist's name belongs to a model while the article belongs to another hand; the authority of
the finished page belongs, as such things generally do, to the office that printed it. Yet the
exceptions matter. Miya Campbell writes on her own magical studies, and Cinna Moan's *Ask Cinna*
belongs to Cinna herself. A reader who treats every contributor as a decorative fiction has merely
accepted the ministry's tidiest story in reverse.

The more usual arrangement is not a fraud exactly. It is a small administrative pageant, in which
several people are asked to lend one another credibility. The writer lends instruction. The model
lends attention. The ministry lends its seal. The reader is asked to accept the package as an
education without wondering too closely who has been paid to speak, who has been paid to appear, and
who has been permitted neither office.

Perhaps that is no more compromised than any other public form of learning. A naval chart may make a
sea route appear inevitable because the surveyor did not record the reef that defeated them. A
mining report may call a mountain a resource because the persons who carry the ore do not sign it.
The clean page is often the last stage of a rather untidy negotiation.

I do not object to spectacle. Spectacle has fed merchants, crowned rulers, sold patent remedies,
and occasionally carried a useful fact past a locked door. I object only to the lazy claim that
spectacle makes its own ethics. It does not. A clever publication may be instructive, vulgar,
exploitative, liberating, or several of these things in an afternoon. The reader deserves more than
a lecture on modesty from people who have arranged the lights so carefully.

Whether *Legally Bare* succeeds as public education remains to be seen. It is at least candid enough
to reveal the terms of the experiment. That is more than can be said of most institutions, and less
than ought to be required of any of them.
