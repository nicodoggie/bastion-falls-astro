## What You'll Learn

This lesson teaches how Early Hick points to things in the world:

- endings for "this" and "that";
- the difference between ordinary things and living or forceful things;
- how "this person" can stand in for "I" when needed;
- how plural people forms can mean groups;
- how distant forms can sound like gossip about outsiders.

## Pointing with Endings

English uses separate words like "this" and "that." Early Hick usually uses
word endings instead.

```txt
mater-is
boat-this
"this boat"
```

```txt
mater-en
boat-that
"that boat"
```

These endings come before the sentence role ending:

```txt
mater-is-es
boat-this-MAIN
"this boat" as the main noun or receiver
```

## Things and Living Forces

Early Hick has different pointing endings for ordinary things and for people,
animals, or forces treated as active.

| Plain idea     | Ordinary thing | Living or forceful thing |
| -------------- | -------------- | ------------------------ |
| this           | `-is`          | `-'is`                   |
| that known one | `-en`          | `-tan`                   |
| that sensed one | `-eth`        | `-thir`                  |
| that distant one | `-um`        | `-rum`                   |

People and animals usually use the living/forceful side.

```txt
'al-'is-es
person-this-MAIN
"this person"
```

```txt
pel-tan-es
bear-that-MAIN
"that bear"
```

Some non-living things can also use the living/forceful side when speakers
treat them as active on their own: lightning, wind, seas, rivers, storms, fire,
and other forces with apparent independent motion.

```txt
toran-thir-es
river-that.sensed.force-MAIN
"that river" / "that sensed force of the river"
```

A windmill could be treated this way if it feels like it acts on its own. A
waterwheel is less likely, because the moving water is the obvious force.

## This Person for "I"

Early Hick does not need an English-style "I" in every sentence. If context
makes the speaker clear, the sentence can leave that reference out.

```txt
barak'er
walk-ACTION
"I walk." / "they walk." / "someone walks." depending on context
```

When the speaker needs to emphasize themself, they can use `'al'is`, "this
person."

```txt
'al'is-es barak'er
person-this-MAIN walk-ACTION
"I walk." / "This person walks."
```

This is useful for contrast:

```txt
'al'is-es barak'er, 'al-tan-es imeresp'er
person-this-MAIN walk-ACTION person-that-MAIN sleep-ACTION
"I walk; that person sleeps."
```

## Known People and Distant People

Use `-tan` for a known person or group in the shared conversation.

```txt
'al-tan-el ward'er
person-that-DOER guide-ACTION
"that person guides"
```

Use `ma-` when you mean a group of people. With person words, these forms can
feel like collectivizers: a way to talk about people as a group.

```txt
ma-'al-tan-el ward'er
GROUP-person-that-DOER guide-ACTION
"those people guide" / "that known group guides"
```

Use `-rum` for people who are far away, unknown, generalized, or socially
distant.

```txt
ma-'al-rum-el ward'er
GROUP-person-distant-DOER guide-ACTION
"those people guide" / "people like that guide"
```

That last meaning can appear in gossip about an outgroup. It is like English
"they say" or "those people," where the speaker does not mean a visible group
standing nearby.

## Objects Near You

When you talk about an ordinary object in relation to yourself, use the ordinary
thing endings, not the person forms.

```txt
mater-is-es
boat-this-MAIN
"this boat" / "this boat of mine here"
```

```txt
mater-en-es
boat-that-MAIN
"that boat" / "that boat associated with the current scene"
```

These are not the same as English "my" and "your." They point to something in
the situation.

## Try It

Choose the best form.

1. Say "this boat" as the main noun.
1. Say "that known person" as the doer.
1. Say "those known people" as the doer.
1. Which ending would fit a sensed active wind: `-eth` or `-thir`?
1. Which phrase can sound like gossip about an outgroup: `ma'altanel` or
   `ma'alrumel`?
